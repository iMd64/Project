Organisational Strategy & Strategy Deployment (Slides 04–11)

Slide 04 Figure 3.1

How Organisational Strategy Reaches Project Tasks (Figure 3.1)

How to Read It Read downwards: vision becomes strategy, portfolios, programmes, project strategy and tasks. Read upwards: reports and opportunities inform higher-level choices. Capability and capacity constrain what the organisation can undertake.
Slide 05 Mission Statement

A Mission Should Guide Real Choices

Vague Ambition

"Our mission is to be the best producer in the world of high-technology solutions which delight our customers."

More Useful Focus

"To be the most responsive supplier of PCBs and associated design services to our customers."

Teaching Note The small electronics firm competed through responsiveness to nearby customers. A useful mission reflects a credible advantage and directs decisions. PCB means printed circuit board.
Slide 06 Figure 3.2

Strategy Is a Two-Way Process (Figure 3.2)

Traditional Approach (One-Way Downward)

Weak link between project and organisational strategy, lack of coordination between projects, inevitable resource conflicts, project managers 'minimise negative potential of projects'.

Strategic Approach (Two-Way Interactive)

Coherent, coordinated, focused, strategy-driven, contributing to strategy-forming and strategy deployment; strategic competence in project management provides a source of competitive advantage.

How to Read It The traditional approach has a weak connection between organisational and project strategy. The strategic approach combines direction from leaders with feedback about progress, workload, capability and emerging opportunities.
Slide 07 Strategic Analysis Tools

Tools Inform Strategy; Priorities Make It Actionable

Environment (SWOT & Porter's 5-Forces)

SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats) and analysis of 5-Forces:

  1. Bargaining Power of Customers (Buyers)
  2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers
  3. Threat of New Entrants
  4. Threat of Substitutes
  5. Competitive Rivalry Among Existing Players

Priorities

'What should we prioritise?' — doing too many things risks spreading resources too thinly; clear priorities help managers focus.

Teaching Note SWOT and the five forces provide inputs to strategic thinking. They do not remove the need to choose. Use evidence about the organisation's position and vulnerabilities to decide what deserves scarce resources.
Slide 08 Core Definition

Project Strategy Concerns Value and Direction

"Project strategy is the definition of position, the means and the guidelines of what to do and how to do it, to achieve the highest competitive advantage and the best value from a project."
Teaching Note A schedule explains when work will happen. Project strategy sets the position, means and guidelines for achieving value. It should influence choices about scope, delivery methods, suppliers and priorities.
Slide 09 Figure 3.3

Two Routes from Strategy to Projects (Figure 3.3)

Adapted from Shenhar, A.J., Milosevic, D., Dvir, D. and Thamhain, H., 2007, Linking Project Management to Business Strategy, Project Management Institute, Newtown, PA.

Slide 11 Classroom Activity

Activity: Turn an Objective into a Project

1. Interpret

Teaching scenario: A student-services team wants to reduce the average time to answer student enquiries. Identify the intended benefit and a measure of it.

2. Deploy

Propose one project and two activities that contribute to that objective. Explain the causal link from each activity to the benefit.

3. Test Coherence

Name one other objective the project might harm (e.g., answer quality or staff workload). Propose a check to prevent that conflict.

Teaching Note Deliverable: objective → project → two activities → measure, plus one potential conflict. This is a classroom scenario, not a statement of university policy.

Portfolio & Programme Management (Slides 12–16)

Slide 12 – Slide 13 Portfolios vs. Programmes

Portfolio Management vs. Programme Management

Portfolio Management: Choose What to Do (Slide 12)

"The second principle is that 'it is not possible to do everything'. In practice, organisations and individuals have to make choices about what they do, not just how well they are going to do it. This is part of the strategy process known as portfolio management."

Teaching Note: A portfolio view considers the collection of work and its contribution to organisational priorities. An attractive project may still need to wait if higher-priority work requires the same scarce people or funding.

Programme Management: Obtain Combined Benefits (Slide 13)

"An [organisational] framework for grouping existing or defining new projects, and for focusing all the activities required to achieve a set of major benefits. These projects are managed in a coordinated way, either to achieve a common goal, or to extract benefits which would otherwise not be realised if they were managed independently."

Teaching Note: The focus is coordination across related work so that the combined benefit is realised. Size alone does not reliably distinguish a project from a programme.

Slide 14 Visual Case Example

Completed Projects Can Still Fail to Fit Together

How to Read It The book shows a completed road and utility poles standing in its path (Courtesy of Michael Trolove). Each piece of work can be "finished" while the combined result is unsuitable. Programme management addresses interfaces and shared benefits.
Slide 15 Figure 3.4

Projects in Programmes Can Be Arranged Differently (Figure 3.4)

How to Read It A chain sequences projects; the middle arrangement (portfolio) places projects alongside one another; a network highlights interconnections. Organisations may combine these patterns. Identify the actual dependencies.
Slide 16 Operations vs. Projects

Transformation Must Coexist with Everyday Operations

"It is not possible to shut down normal bank operations to enable work to be done; the bank functions must remain seamless to the customer at all times."
Teaching Note The bank case combines branch redesign and internet banking while normal services continue. Later projects can be refined at programme level as learning develops. Business-as-usual work must be included when estimating available staff time.

Aggregate Resource Planning & Project Selection (Slides 17–25)

Slide 17 – Slide 18 Aggregate Resource Plan & Capacity Math

Aggregate Resource Plans Make Capacity Visible

"Providing a concise statement of the resource requirements of the expected activities, both project and operational, is the objective of the aggregate resource plan."

Combine demand from projects and ongoing operations. Then compare it with available capacity by time period and skill. Adding all hours together is insufficient if the missing capacity belongs to a specialist role.

Worked Example: Available Hours Are Finite (Slide 18)

"This gave a capacity figure of 1200 hours per person per year."

Teaching Note These are assumptions used by the engineering consultancy in the book. They allow for leave, training, meetings, administration and other demands. Use evidence for your own organisation rather than assuming every paid hour is project capacity.
Slide 19 – Slide 20 Worked Capacity Calculation

Activity & Worked Answer: Is the Proposed Workload Feasible?

Teaching Scenario (Slide 19)

  • Team size: 4 people × 1,200 productive hours/year each
  • Ongoing operations need: 1,800 hours
  • Proposed Projects: Project A = 1,100 hrs, Project B = 900 hrs, Project C = 1,300 hrs
  • Task: Find total capacity, capacity remaining for projects, total project demand, and the capacity gap.

Worked Answer: The Portfolio Exceeds Capacity (Slide 20)

  • Total available hours: 4 × 1,200 = 4,800 hours
  • Hours left after operations: 4,800 − 1,800 = 3,000 hours
  • Hours required by A + B + C: 1,100 + 900 + 1,300 = 3,300 hours
  • Result: 3,300 − 3,000 = 300-hour shortfall
4 × 1,200 − 1,800 = 3,000 available  |  3,300 − 3,000 = 300-hour shortfall
Teaching Note All three cannot be delivered under the stated assumptions. Options include deferring or reducing work, or adding suitable capacity. Choose using strategic contribution, deadlines, dependencies, skill needs and cost — not by selecting the smallest project automatically.
Slide 21 Work in Progress (WIP)

More Active Projects Can Mean Less Completed Work

"For the development organisation, a move away from the old system of 'whoever shouts loudest' to a clear system of priorities reduced the workload from 72 projects to 12."
Teaching Note The book reports that clearer priorities and less work in progress helped this firm complete twice as many projects that year with the same resources. Treat this as one case, not a universal ratio. Switching and conflicting priorities consume capacity.

Project Governance & The Project Management Office (PMO) (Slides 26–33)

Slide 26 – Slide 27 Governance & Figure 3.5

Governance: Responsibility, Steering & Basic Structure (Figure 3.5)

"Project governance sets out who is responsible for doing what and to whom they are responsible."

Governance defines how direction, authority and accountability work. Some problems exceed the project manager's authority, such as resolving competition for resources across departments. The project needs a route for those decisions.

How to Read It Follow the connections from programme management → project board → project manager → PMO and teams. Technical advice and assurance support the board.
Slide 28 – Slide 29 Governance Roles Defined

Board Interests & Management, Advice and Assurance Roles

SRO and Sponsor (Slide 28)

The SRO (Senior Responsible Owner) is the person who ultimately wants the result. The sponsor has the budget for the project to be carried out.

Senior User (Slide 28)

Provides high-level input from those who will ultimately be using the output of the project (e.g. users of an IT system).

Senior Supplier (Slide 28)

The representative of those who will be delivering the project.

Project Manager (Slide 29)

Coordinates day-to-day delivery and the teams doing the work. Escalates decisions that exceed delegated authority.

Technical Advisory Group (Slide 29)

Provides specialist advice to the board on the technical direction and viability of the work.

Project Assurance (Slide 29)

Provides confidence that important requirements and controls are being met. Placed outside the project manager's direct control.

Teaching Note Identify who wants the result, who controls funding, who represents users and who represents delivery. Advice informs a decision; assurance examines whether the project meets relevant expectations.
Slide 30 – Slide 32 Figure 3.6 & PMO Roles

The PMO and the Project Exchange Support and Learning (Figure 3.6)

Five Categories of PMO Work (Slide 31)

PMO Category Selected Roles & Responsibilities
1. Performance Report status to upper management; monitor and control project performance.
2. Capabilities Develop competency of personnel, including training.
3. Multiple projects Allocate resources between projects.
4. Strategy Provide advice to senior management; participate in strategic planning.

Slide 32: "All this additional support to the project process does not come free."
Teaching Note (Slides 31–32) Training, coordination, reporting and learning need resources. Assess the benefit of support against its cost and administrative demands; more reporting is not automatically better management.
Slide 33 Governance Activity

Activity: Design Governance for the Careers Event

1. Allocate Roles

Identify a sponsor, user representative, delivery lead and project manager. State each person's decision responsibility.

2. Plan Escalation

The venue becomes unavailable and an alternative exceeds the agreed budget. Who proposes options, who reviews impacts and who authorises the decision?

3. Choose Support

Identify one PMO-style service that would help and one record worth keeping for the next event.

Teaching Note Suggested approach: The manager assembles options and impacts; the authorised sponsor/board decides within governance arrangements. Booking records, templates and lessons support future teams.

Slide 37 – Slide 39 Strategic Retrospective & Workshop

Technical Achievement vs. Commercial Value & Case Workshop

"Despite the magnificence of the aircraft and the hugely impressive engineering and manufacturing behind it, the case shows the difficulty of predicting how a multi-decade investment will play out in a competitive market."

The chapter records Airbus's 2019 announcement to end production and discusses market uncertainty and the pandemic. Its lesson is that engineering achievement does not guarantee the expected investment return.

Case Workshop & Suggested Analysis (Slides 38–39)

1. Programme Perspective

Treat development as a programme: related engineering, manufacturing and logistics projects contribute to a common aircraft capability. Coordinated interfaces and milestones are essential.

2. Governance and PMO

A proposed board brings business (SRO/sponsor), user (airlines/passengers) and supplier interests together. Technical advice and assurance inform decisions; the PMO coordinates dependencies and shared information.

3. Residual Strategic Risk

Market demand and investment assumptions can change over a long development period. Good coordination reduces delivery problems but cannot guarantee commercial success.

Exit Check: Connect Choice, Capacity and Accountability (Slide 40)

Slide 40 Exam Self-Test & Model Answers

Three Core Exam Questions

Question 1

How is choosing a portfolio different from coordinating a programme?

Portfolio management selects work across the organisation for strategic value within scarce capacity, whereas programme management coordinates related projects to realise combined benefits and manage interfaces.

Question 2

Why must an aggregate resource plan include ongoing operations?

Because ongoing operations (business-as-usual) consume the same finite capacity and specialist staff hours as project work.

Question 3

What decision might require a sponsor or board, rather than the project manager alone?

A change beyond delegated budget or scope, or resolving a cross-project / cross-departmental priority and resource conflict.
