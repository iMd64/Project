Defining a Project & Its Core Characteristics

Slides 05–12 • Why "Start and Finish" Is Not Enough, 4 Professional Definitions, Unique/Temporary/Focused, Mission & Benefits, Emergence vs. Uncertainty, Social Construction & Fig 1.1

Slide 05 Limits of the Basic Definition

A start and finish are not enough

📖 Book Excerpt Exact Wording

"One step on from this is the most basic of accepted definitions: a project is a task that has a beginning and an end. This is insufficient, as the two examples below will demonstrate."

Example 1: Food inspection (Too narrow/repetitive)

Repeated operational work. A routine inspection starts and finishes, but repeats an established process.

Example 2: Ship's whole life (Too broad)

Multiple projects and ongoing activities. A ship's 60-year life contains many different projects and operations (design, build, refits, daily voyages, decommissioning).

🎓 Teaching Note Choose a useful unit of work before choosing a management approach.
Slide 06 Slide 07
Four Formal Definitions of a Project

Professional definitions & Environment / Organisation views

Slide 06 • Definition 1 (Change & Objectives)

"A unique, transient endeavour undertaken to bring about change and to achieve planned objectives."

Slide 06 • Definition 2 (Product, Service or Result)

"A project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result."

Slide 07 • Definition 3 (Management Environment)

"Temporary management environment, usually undertaken in phases, created for the purpose of delivering one or more business outputs or outcomes."

Slide 07 • Definition 4 (Temporary Organisation)

"A temporary organisation that is created for the purpose of delivering one or more business products according to an agreed business case."

Slide 08 Unique • Temporary • Focused

Three common themes

1. Unique The exact project has not been performed before. Has a degree of novelty in time, place, team, product or service.
2. Temporary When finished, the team moves on. Financial resources are temporary and almost always finite—when completed, funding ceases.
3. Focused Task is to deliver a particular product, service or result (the specific mission), even if not every detail is clear at the start.
⚠️ Teaching Note (Slide 08 – Exam Trap): Unique does NOT mean that every activity is new. Temporary describes the delivery effort; its benefits may last much longer.
Slide 09 What vs. Why

Mission and benefits

📖 Exact Book Quote

"It is useful to look further than this, with the mission being a means to an end. We say that projects are undertaken to deliver benefits. This characteristic is evident in both commercial and non-commercial projects."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 09): A mission explains what the project is trying to achieve. Benefits explain why the result matters. In the book's relief example, the benefit is saving lives; the immediate work (mission) may include medical treatment or shelter.
Slide 10 Crucial Exam Distinction

Emergence is different from uncertainty

1. Emergence (Requirements) High-level benefits were known (save lives), but exact objectives and means to achieve them could be determined only once a certain amount of work had been done.
2. Uncertainty (Environment) Where emergence referred to the requirements of the project, uncertainty covers all of the environmental conditions in which a project has to operate.
🎓 Teaching Example (Slide 10): Discovering that residents need shelter changes your understanding of requirements (emergence). Not knowing when supplies will arrive is uncertainty about delivery conditions.
Slide 11 Social Construction & Integration

Projects are social and integrating

📖 Exact Book Quote

"We say that unlike a machine that is real and tangible, a project is a social construction — it was literally devised by people. It involves people and systems of people, both in the project team and associated with the project as customers, for instance. It is intangible."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 11 – Electric-Vehicle Example): Connects vehicles, charging, servicing and users. Technical performance alone cannot make the change work: people, organisations and supporting systems must fit together.

Process Thinking & Volume vs. Variety

Slides 13–17 • System of Delivery, Fig 1.2 (Volume vs. Variety Spectrum), 24-Hour Modular McDonald's Restaurant Case & Work Classification

Slide 13 Slide 14 • Figure 1.2
Process Thinking & Volume vs. Variety Spectrum

A project can be studied as a process & Volume, variety and three types of project (Figure 1.2)

📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 13)

"Further description of a project comes from analysing the system of delivery, including consideration of how activities in the project are identified, planned and executed, and how issues such as change and uncertainty are handled by the project team."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 13) Process thinking asks how work is identified, planned and executed, and how the team responds when circumstances change. It makes delivery practices visible so they can be improved.
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 14) Volume means throughput; variety means the different process variations. • First-timers have high novelty.
• "As ... but ..." work resembles previous work with changes.
• "Painting by numbers" has a well-known process and outcome.
• Point A marks routine inspections; Point B marks the whole ship lifecycle. Moving up and left toward repeatable processes brings reduced risk and uncertainty.
Slide 15 Real World Process Thinking

Original example: building a restaurant in 24–48 hours

• It is possible to build a McDonald's drive-through restaurant in just 24 hours (one went from 'clear site' to 'open for business' in less than 48 hours, including foundations).

• Built by contractors Yorkon and Britspace (over 300 times in the UK alone).

• Instead of treating each site as a slow, unique build taking months, they made the building modular (typically 6 modules manufactured off-site and assembled on-site), improving the process each time.

"Recognising that this was likely to be a project that was repeated meant that it was worth the companies investing in finding ways to improve the process."
🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 15): The lesson is to identify what can be reused, even when each location is different.
Slide 16 Slide 17
Work Classification Activity

Activity & Debrief: Classification changes management

Example Work Classification (Fig 1.2) Management Implication
1. Routine food inspections Operations The same visit–report–follow-up process is repeated.
2. Disease-outbreak response Project (As ... but ...) A rare response with its own characteristics, supported by advance preparation.

2. Software Development of the software – the user interface, and the firmware.
3. Production Production set-up – tooling up to produce prototypes to final product and upgrades.
4. Ecosystem Apps, music, films, a network of content creators – all of these add value.
5. Marketing From identification of market needs to promotion of the product.

Slide 23: General management vs. project management

General management Project management
Responsible for managing the current situation Responsible for overseeing change
Authority defined by management structure Lines of authority 'fuzzy'
Consistent set of tasks Ever-changing set of tasks
Responsibility limited to their own function Responsibility for cross-functional activities
Main task is optimisation Main task is the resolution of conflict
Limited set of variables Contains intrinsic uncertainties

Slide 28 Slide 29
Historical Perspective & The 5 Generations of PM
📖 Slide 28: Why Look at the History?

"History should provide us with lessons that we can draw upon to improve our management of the present and the future... Projects are rarely totally unique and should avoid repeating the mistakes of the past."

⚠️ Teaching Note (Slide 28 – Survivor Bias) The chapter cautions against simple comparisons with ancient projects: resources, timescales and expectations differed, and successful survivors are easier to see than failures. History should support learning, not imitation.

Slide 29: Five generations of project management

Planning tools and large engineering projects

1990s
Second generation

Standards and wider professional recognition

Teaching Note (Slide 29): These are overlapping developments, not five mutually exclusive methods from which to choose one.

Slide 30 Slide 31 Slide 32
Project Integrators, Context Fit & The 3 Structure Traps
📖 Slide 30: Project Managers as Integrators

"In this new approach, project managers became project integrators, responsible for integrating the required resources, knowledge and processes from the project's beginning to end."

📖 Slide 31: No Single Approach Fits Every Setting

"One single approach to managing projects is never going to fit with this diversity of settings in which work is carried out." (Uncertainty, outsourcing, virtual teams, limited direct authority).

Slide 32: Persistent traps — too little or too much structure

1. "Ready, fire, aim."

A project is started with no clear objectives (no shared direction).

2. "It's all in my head."

The project manager sets out with all the information in their head (dangerous dependence on one person).

3. "Project management – we have a procedure for that."

Decision making can be very slow and the overhead costs associated with such systems significant (excessive bureaucracy).

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 32): Adapt the level of formalisation to the project.
Slide 33 • Qualifications

Knowing a method ≠ applying it

"Such courses are valuable as first steps on the way to becoming a professional project manager... however, they are only a starting point."

Separates acquiring knowledge from applying it well in context.

Slide 34 • Research & Project X

Research improves practice

"It offers insight into, and explanations of, processes and outcomes. Additionally, it helps practitioners conceptualise their work differently, and think more critically."

Project X (p. 18) studies value, practices, data, assurance, capability and transformation.

Slide 35 • Concept Check

Check: Adapt without losing direction

Prompt: "Requirements will emerge, so we do not need objectives or a shared plan." Sound conclusion?

Suggested Response: No! An initial mission and desired benefits can guide action while detailed requirements develop. Revisit plans as evidence changes.

Case Workshop: Four Different Managers

Slides 36–43 • Housing Development, Total Quality Management (TQM), Financial IT Systems & Government Trade Portfolio

Slide 37 • Case 1 Site / Project Manager

Case 1: Housing development

"Most of the work is supervisory, ensuring that orders are placed and materials arrive on time, people turn up, do the job properly and get paid for it at the end of the week. There are always arguments between the various tradespeople to resolve and problems just get dumped on the desk."
🎓 Context: Takes buildings from drawings and schedules to handover. Focuses on materials, tradespeople, quality and daily problems.
Slide 38 • Case 2 Change Leader → Sponsor

Case 2: Introducing Total Quality Management (TQM)

"The complexity came because the project would hopefully change the way that everyone in the company thought and worked [i.e. both attitude and procedures]. This would have to be done through consultation, training and the demonstration through piloting small-scale improvement activities, that the move towards TQ was worthwhile."
🎓 Context: Quality Director begins with an audit, uses training and pilots, then cascades learning and develops into sponsoring improvement subprojects.
Slide 39 • Case 3 Programme Manager

Case 3: Financial management systems

"This was a manager responsible for a large-scale IT change. She was in charge of a multi-year piece of work with numerous interlinked IT projects that needed to be completed to bring about an update and consolidation of her organisation's (rather old and previously fragmented) IT systems."
🎓 Context: Includes system design, network upgrades, support, training, monitoring and security. Must coordinate internal teams, suppliers and users.
Slide 40 • Case 4 Portfolio Manager

Case 4: Government trade portfolio

"The commitment to a particular condition with one country may preclude a deal with another. So, while one project or negotiation in the portfolio might be important, it needed to align with the others, both current and future."
🎓 Context: Post-EU exit trade negotiations portfolio facing a shortage of negotiators and the need to establish support capacity.

Suggested Analysis: Comparing All Four Management Roles (Slides 42–43)

Case Possible Role Title What Must Be Coordinated Desirable Characteristics & Case Evidence
1. Housing development Site / project manager Coordinate trades, materials and task dependencies from drawings to handover. Clear communication, practical judgement and conflict resolution. Evidence: late/incomplete work delays others; daily tradespeople arguments.
2. Quality transformation (TQM) Change-project leader (developing into sponsor) Coordinate attitudes, procedures, training and improvement subprojects. Influence and patience. Evidence: adoption across the company requires consultation, training and pilot demonstration.

Exit Check: Explain, Do Not Just Name

Slide 44 • Final Exam Self-Test & Complete Suggested Answers

Slide 44 • Final Review Three High-Yield Exam Questions
1. Why does a beginning and an end not prove that work is a project?

➔ Suggested Answer: Because routine operational work (such as a food inspection) also starts and ends, and a multi-decade asset lifecycle (such as a ship's 60-year life) contains many separate projects and operations. You must consider the nature of the task (uniqueness/novelty, temporary organisation/funding, and focused mission/change).

2. How can a unique project still use a repeated delivery process?

➔ Suggested Answer: Circumstances, time, team, or site location can differ while the underlying delivery methods and modules are reused and improved (as in "painting by numbers" projects like the modular 24-hour McDonald's restaurant builds).

3. Why might a project manager need a different approach from a line manager?

➔ Suggested Answer: Because temporary, cross-functional project work oversees change rather than maintaining the status quo, introducing changing tasks, 'fuzzy' lines of authority, cross-departmental conflict resolution, and intrinsic uncertainties.
