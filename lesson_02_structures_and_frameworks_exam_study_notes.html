The Project Environment: The 5-C Model & PESTEL

Slides 05–09 • Figure 2.1 (The 5-C Model), Exact Definitions of the 5 Cs, Full PESTEL Breakdown & Completeness vs. Customer Focus

Slide 05 • Figure 2.1 Describing the project environment: the 5-C model

The project environment: the 5-C model (Figure 2.1)

🔍 How to Read It (Slide 05) The project team sits inside a wider environment. The two-way arrows matter: external conditions shape the project, and the project can change those conditions. Use the five Cs to ask what influences delivery.
Slide 06 Exact Textbook Definitions

The five Cs: exact definitions

The 5 Cs Exact Definition
1. Context the external general influences on the organisation in which the project is taking place;
2. Complexity the level of difficulty or complication of a piece of work called 'a project';
3. Completeness how much of the end requirement a project will deliver;
4. Competitiveness how many other organisations will be competing to deliver that work;
5. Customer focus the expectation that customers will have their needs met by the project.
⚠️ Teaching Note & Exam Trap (Slide 06) The headings prompt different questions. Completeness concerns the breadth of the solution; it is NOT a percentage-complete measure.
Slide 07 Slide 08 Slide 09
PESTEL Analysis & Complete Solutions

PESTEL: Examining the External Context (Slides 07–08)

P – Political (Slide 07) All organisations have politics, hierarchies and a requirement at least to recognise where the power in that organisation really lies.
E – Economic (Slide 07) The influence of general and local economics (e.g., supplier prices, funding climate).
S – Social (Slide 07) The influence of social changes on the project environment, for instance on methods of communication, use of social media, and the impact on relationships.
T – Technical (Slide 08) Changes in technology will challenge the viability of some projects and create the need for others, in addition to determining the way in which those projects are delivered.
E – Environmental (Slide 08) An assessment of environmental impact is mandated for many projects now as part of corporate social responsibility.
L – Legal (Slide 08) Recent challenges have included data-sharing (GDPR) and uncertainty over international trade rules.
🎓 Teaching Notes (Slides 07–08 – Campus Event Application)

• Slide 07: For a campus event, consider approval priorities, supplier prices and how students communicate. Record a possible effect on the project, not just a PESTEL label.

• Slide 08: Test registration technology, consider waste and transport impacts, and identify applicable permissions and data-handling requirements.

📖 Slide 09: Complete Solution & Customer-Focused Process

"Customer focus applies to both the outcome and the process by which it is delivered."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 09 – Flight-Training Example) The flight-training example expands from selling simulators to providing instructors, aircraft and facilities. Completeness broadens the solution. Customer focus involves users during delivery, so the result meets their needs.

The Project 7-S Framework (Internal Assessment)

Slides 10–14 • Table 2.1 Project 7-S Adaptation, Direction/Organisation/Methods, People/Capability/Relationships & Student Careers Event Review

Slide 10 • Table 2.1 Slide 11 Slide 12
Maylor & Turner's Project 7-S Framework

The 7-S examines the project itself (Table 2.1)

📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 10)

"Their original 7-S is amended for the project environment, with a description of each of the elements, as shown in Table 2.1."

⚠️ Exam Trap Warning (Slide 10) Use this book's project version: Strategy, Structure, Systems, Staff, Skills, Style/culture and Stakeholders. It is an adaptation of McKinsey's framework; do not substitute a different seven-item list (e.g., McKinsey's "Shared Values" is replaced by Stakeholders in the project 7-S).
Group 7-S Element Exact Textbook Definition (Slides 11–12) Key Guiding Question
Direction, Organisation & Methods (Slide 11) 1. Strategy The high-level requirements of the project and the means to achieve them What are we trying to achieve, and how?
2. Structure The organisational arrangement that will be used to carry out the project Who reports to whom? Who owns decisions?
3. Systems The methods for work to be designed, monitored and controlled How are progress, quality and changes handled?
People, Capability & Relationships (Slide 12) 4. Staff The selection, recruitment, management and leadership of those working on the project Do we have the right people allocated?
5. Skills The managerial and technical tools available to the project manager and the staff, and how these are developed Do they have the capability & tools needed?
6. Style / culture The underlying way of working and inter-relating within the work team or organisation How does the team collaborate and surface issues?
7. Stakeholders Individuals and groups with an interest in the project process or outcome Who is affected by the process or result?
🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 12): Distinguish having people (Staff) from having the necessary capability (Skills). Culture shapes how they work together; stakeholders include those affected by the process or result.
Slide 13 Slide 14
Worked Class Activity: 6-Week Student Careers Event

Activity: Review a student careers event & Suggested 7-S review

Teaching Scenario (Slide 13): A student team will host a careers event in six weeks. Employers attend, the venue is shared and the booking system is untested. Identify a concern under each S and propose practical actions.
1. Direction and delivery (Slide 14)
  • • Strategy: Define the event's benefit.
  • • Structure: Name decision owners.
  • • Systems: Confirm venue access and test booking before registration opens.
2. People and relationships (Slide 14)
  • • Staff: Allocate reliable volunteers.
  • • Skills: Provide technical support.
  • • Style/culture: Surface issues early.
  • • Stakeholders: Agree expectations with employers and students.
🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 14) An action can address several Ss. A booking rehearsal, for example, checks the system, reveals skill gaps and involves users (stakeholders).

ICOM: The Project as a Conversion Process

Slides 15–20 • Figure 2.2 (ICOM Model), Inputs/Brief vs. Outputs/Changed People, 9 Controls/Constraints, 5 Mechanisms & Careers Event ICOM Map

Slide 15 • Figure 2.2 The project as a conversion process

ICOM: the project as a conversion process (Figure 2.2)

🔍 How to Read It (Slide 15)
  • • Inputs express a want or need.
  • • Outputs satisfy that need.
  • • Controls and constraints limit delivery.
  • • Mechanisms make it possible.

Read the arrows before listing examples: each has a different function.

Slide 16 Inputs & Outputs

Inputs and outputs: need and transformation

1. Input and brief: There has to be a limit to the number of projects started, encapsulated into a brief – a document describing the nature of the work to be undertaken before resources are released to do even preliminary work.
2. Output: changed people: Through a training project, the participants have received new knowledge and so are part of the transformation process as well as being a product of it.
🎓 Teaching Note: A project can produce information, a tangible product or changed capability. For a training project, completing sessions is not enough to demonstrate that the intended learning need has been met.
Slide 17 9 Controls & Constraints

Controls and constraints go beyond a deadline

1. Time: All projects by definition have a time constraint; in practice, often found to be the most challenging to meet.
2. Cost: The value and timing of financial resources required to carry out the project work.
3. Quality: The standards by which both the product (output) and the process itself will be judged.
🎓 Teaching Note: The book also lists legal, ethical, environmental, logic, activation and indirect-effect constraints (e.g., an earlier task must finish [logic] or formal authorisation is required [activation]).
Slide 18 5 Mechanisms & Integration

Mechanisms: how the transformation becomes possible

📖 Exact Book Quote

"They are required to be the integrator of the elements of the project – the need or want with the available mechanisms or resources – under the conditions imposed by the constraints."

5 Listed Mechanisms: (1) people, (2) knowledge and expertise, (3) financial resources, (4) tools and techniques, and (5) technology.
⚠️ Dual Role of Funding (Slides 18 & 20): A budget ceiling constrains work (Control/Constraint); available funding enables it (Mechanism).
Slide 19 Slide 20
Worked Class Activity: Careers Event ICOM Map

Activity & Suggested ICOM Map for the Student Careers Event

1. Input

Students need access to employers and clearer information about career opportunities.

2. Output

A delivered event that enables useful employer-student interaction and provides accessible follow-up information. (Avoid naming only the event date!)

3. Controls / constraints

Six-week deadline, agreed spending limit, venue conditions and relevant permissions.

4. Mechanisms

Student organisers, employer contacts, venue staff, available funding and a tested booking platform.

Project Lifecycles: 4-D, Activity & S-Curves, Stage Gates, Agile & Design Thinking

Slides 21–30 • Fig 2.3 (4-D Lifecycle), Hedgehog Syndrome, Fig 2.4 (Activity Curve) vs. Fig 2.5 (S-Curve), Fig 2.6 (IDeA Stage-Gate Model), Project vs. Product Life, Agile Values, Hybrid & Design Thinking

Slide 21 • Figure 2.3 Slide 22 Slide 23
Four Phases of Project Lifecycle (4-D) & Avoiding "Hedgehog Syndrome"

4-D: connect delivery with learning (Figure 2.3)

D1 – Define (➔ Output: The brief): What is to be done? Why is it to be done? What are the benefits?
D2 – Design (➔ Output: The proposal/PID): How will it be done? Who will be involved in each part? When can it start and finish? What methods and structure are going to be used?
D3 – Deliver / Do (➔ Output: The outcomes): How should the project be managed, measured and evaluated on a day-to-day basis?
D4 – Develop (➔ Output: Process & product knowledge): How can we learn from the work? How can the project process be improved?
📖 Slide 23 Quote: Avoid "Hedgehog Syndrome"

"Unless there is an opportunity to develop the project processes and provide the feedback to the organisation, the knowledge is lost."

⚠️ Teaching Note (Slide 23 – Lessons Identified vs. Learned) The book describes organisations repeating expensive mistakes because experience does not feed into future projects (like hedgehogs getting flattened on roads repeatedly). A review must lead to changed practice. Recording "lessons identified" alone does not prove that learning has happened.
Slide 24 • Figure 2.4 Level of activity vs. Time

Activity rises and falls over the lifecycle (Figure 2.4)

🔍 How to Read It (Slide 24) The horizontal axis is time; the vertical axis is activity level. In the generic model, delivery ("Do it") has the highest activity, then activity falls during "Develop it". This is a conceptual pattern, not measured data or a timetable for every project.
Slide 25 • Figure 2.5 Cumulative expenditure vs. Time (S-curve)

The S-curve shows cumulative expenditure (Figure 2.5)

🔍 How to Read It (Slide 25 – Exam Favorite) Cumulative spending adds up over time, so the curve does NOT fall when activity declines. Its slope is steepest during intensive delivery ("Do it"). Early decisions can commit later spending even when little cash has yet been paid.

Slide 27 Slide 28
Product Life & Agile Values

Slide 27: Project lifecycle and product life are different

"It is important to distinguish between the 'project' lifecycle (the development and delivery of the project itself) and that of the product or service being developed."

Teaching Note: A project develops and delivers something; the resulting asset or service may operate for years. The hospital-system example includes handover, training and support. Consider ongoing use and maintenance during early planning.

Slide 28: Agile values — a shift of emphasis

1. People individuals and interactions over processes and tools;
2. Working results working software over comprehensive documentation;
3. Collaboration customer collaboration over contract negotiation;
4. Adaptation responding to change over following a plan.
⚠️ Teaching Note (Slide 28): The book says that the items on the right still have value. Agile is NOT permission to omit planning, documentation or agreements.
Slide 29 Slide 30
Hybrid Working & Design Thinking

Slide 29: Choose linear, iterative or hybrid working

"A mixed or 'hybrid' approach should be considered when some aspects of a project would benefit from an agile approach but other aspects are deemed inappropriate for its application."

Teaching Note: A stable venue-booking process may use planned steps; an unfamiliar registration experience may benefit from prototypes and user feedback. The choice follows the task and uncertainty.

Slide 30: Design thinking — understand before solutionising

"But one of the key differences in approach is the discipline of holding off on a solution, until the problem is properly understood."

Teaching Note: Before deciding that students need a new app, investigate what prevents them from finding opportunities. The book connects user needs, available resources and organisational constraints; creativity applies to both the output and its delivery process.

🎓 Pair Prompt (Slide 30): Propose two questions you would ask students before choosing a technical solution (e.g., "How do you currently hear about employers?" and "What stops you from attending career events?").

Managerial Complexity: 3 Lenses, Crossrail & Bodies of Knowledge

Slides 31–38 • Book Launch vs. 24-Partner Research, Structural / Socio-Political / Emergent Complexity, Complexity Profile, Crossrail Case, Understand-Reduce-Respond & BoKs

Slide 31 Comparing Managerial Demands

Two projects, different managerial demands

1. Book launch (Local bookshop owner) "He would need to liaise between the publishers, the author's agent and his own team to generate interest in the event and to use it to turn that interest into much-needed sales."
➔ Experienced shop owner can use relatively simple planning.
2. Scientific research (European network) "The grant funded a network of scientists to pursue work that would assist in the understanding of major diseases, was worth many million euros per year and involved no less than 24 partners from all over Europe."
➔ Involves many partners, legal agreements and specialist work.
🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 31) Importance alone does not tell us the kind of management support needed. (The book launch is vital to the shop owner's survival, yet its managerial complexity is far lower than the 24-partner research network).
Slide 32 Slide 33 Slide 34 Slide 35
Three Dimensions of Complexity & Dynamic Profiling

The Three Complexity Lenses (Structural, Socio-Political, Emergent)

1. Structural Complexity (Slide 32)
"Structural complexity is associated with size, variety, breadth of scope, the level of interdependence of people or tasks, or the pace of the work."

Teaching Note: Look for many interacting tasks, disciplines, constraints or organisations. A large number is not automatically the problem; the coordination it creates is. Responses: clearer interfaces, roles and dependency tracking.

2. Socio-Political Complexity (Slide 33)
"Socio-political complexity is associated with a project's importance, its people, power, and (P)politics, in both the project team and wider."

Teaching Note: People may want different outcomes or have unequal influence. More scheduling detail will NOT by itself resolve conflicting interests.

3. Emergent Complexity (Slide 34)
"Emergent complexity comprises uncertainty and change. Uncertainty is typically the result of novelty of technology or process, a lack of experience, and (non)availability of information on which to make decisions."

Teaching Note: Test assumptions, use feedback and revisit plans. One event can affect all three complexity types, so categories can overlap.

📖 Slide 35 Quote: Complexity Profile

"A complexity profile is a useful framework if used at the outset and then at various times during the project to identify particular difficulties and formulate a response to them."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 35) A profile identifies particular sources of difficulty; it is not just a single "high/low" label. Assessments are subjective and should be supported by evidence. Recheck them as requirements, relationships and resources change.
Slide 36 Crossrail Case Study

Crossrail: use the three complexity lenses

📖 Exact Book Quote

"Get some trains that will run on the system, work with the signalling to the east of London, the underground, and the west (all completely different)."

• Structural lens: Technical interfaces between tunnels, existing infrastructure, stations, power, 3 incompatible signalling systems and trains.
• Socio-political lens: Different organisations and priorities across London transport bodies and contractors.
• Emergent lens: Unknown underground conditions and software integration delays creating uncertainty.
🎓 Teaching Note: Technical interfaces are structural; different organisations/priorities create socio-political difficulty; unknown underground conditions create uncertainty.
Slide 37 Slide 38
Managing Complexity & BoKs

Slide 37: Understand, reduce, respond

1. Understand Describe the sources of difficulty and their interactions. Gather evidence from people doing and affected by the work.
2. Reduce Remove avoidable difficulty where possible: clarify scope, simplify interfaces or resolve competing requirements.
3. Respond Organise to handle remaining complexity: use expertise, coordination, stakeholder engagement and feedback.
⚠️ Teaching Note (Slide 37): Do not confuse reducing complexity with ignoring it; residual difficulties still need a response.

Slide 38: Bodies of Knowledge (APM / PMI) support judgement

"The standards do not set out to be comprehensive about everything that is known about project management; rather they provide guidance on some of the common ground between projects that covers 'most projects most of the time'."

Case Study: The Arson Task Force (ATF) & Exit Check

Slides 39–45 • 24-Month Multi-Agency Project, Complete 7-S Case Evidence, 2-Year Extension Recommendations & Final Exit Check

Slide 39 Slide 40 Slide 41 Slide 42
Avon Fire & Rescue + Avon & Somerset Constabulary + Somerset Fire & Rescue

Case: The Arson Task Force (ATF) & 7-S Diagnostic Evidence

📖 Slide 39 Case Overview

"The Arson Task Force (ATF) was the name given to a 24-month project to reduce the incidence of arson in one area."

Alliance of two fire and rescue services and an area police force, with eight full-time staff. Work included partnership, detection, education and intervention.

⚠️ Teaching Note (Slide 39) The review reports reduced arson and a strong knowledge base, alongside opportunities to improve management. A successful outcome does NOT prove every management practice was robust.
7-S Element Exact Case Evidence Quote (Slides 40–42) Strength vs. Vulnerability (Teaching Notes)
1. Strategy
(Slide 40)		 "There was no priority attached to these objectives however, and no allowance or process for change and assessing the impact of changes on quality." Objectives provided direction, but unclear priorities and lack of change control created vulnerability.
2. Structure
(Slide 40)		 "While no single individual had overall responsibility or authority for the project, the shared goals and the efforts of the two individuals concerned have carried this." Co-location supported teamwork, but shared/unclear overall authority was a governance risk.
3. Systems
(Slide 41)		 "However, this does have an impact on the repeatability of such a project and does not provide any evidence for future time or resource planning." Flexibility helped the team, but undocumented processes made the approach harder to repeat.
4. Staff
(Slide 41)		 "When a key member of staff was promoted out of the team, a core resource was removed. More importantly for the project was the loss of considerable knowledge as a result of this move, and instability for the team." Knowledge concentrated in individuals became vulnerable when people moved on.
5. Skills
(Slide 42)		 "Not training the project managers in PRINCE2 until well into the execution phase of the project was a major oversight." Late PM training reduced its value during initial planning and setup.
6. Style / culture
(Slide 42)		 "From the comments of the interviewees, the can-do culture of the team appeared to be entirely consistent with the goals of the project and has been encouraged by the team leaders." Major strength to preserve! Constructive, motivated team culture.
7. Stakeholders
(Slide 42)		 "There was no evidence that a communications plan/strategy was in place and this should form an integral part of the work packages in such a project." Missing formal stakeholder communication strategy across agencies and community.
Slide 43 Slide 44
2-Year Funding Extension Recommendations

Workshop: Use the 7-S to recommend improvements for the next two years

📖 Book Workshop Question (Slide 43)

"The ATF received funding for a further two years. What would you recommend that the management of the ATF does to ensure success?"

1. Clarify governance (Strategy & Structure)

Agree priorities, decision authority and board responsibilities. Evidence: no prioritisation and no single overall authority. Retain the benefits of co-location.

2. Transfer knowledge (Systems & Staff)

Record basic plans, progress and role knowledge; prepare for staff changes. Evidence: weak systematisation and knowledge loss when a key person left.

3. Build capability (Skills, Culture & Stakeholders)

Train early and set a stakeholder communication plan. Keep the can-do culture. Monitor both delivery activity and longer-term outcomes.

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 44): A reduction in arson can be affected by external events; process measures help assess the team's work, but do not alone prove social impact.
Slide 45 • Final Review Exit Check: Choose a Model and Explain Why
Question 1: Which model helps examine external political or technical influences? Which reviews the project's internal management issues?

➔ Suggested Answer: PESTEL (within the 5-C Context) examines external political, economic, social, technical, environmental and legal influences; the project 7-S framework (Strategy, Structure, Systems, Staff, Skills, Style/culture, Stakeholders) reviews the project's internal management issues.

Question 2: In ICOM [Inputs, Controls (Constraints), Outputs, and Mechanisms], how does a constraint differ from a mechanism?

➔ Suggested Answer: A constraint limits or governs delivery (e.g., time deadline, budget ceiling, legal/quality standard), whereas a mechanism enables the transformation to happen (e.g., people, expertise, available funding, tools and technology).

Question 3: Why should a complexity assessment be repeated during the project?

➔ Suggested Answer: Because requirements, resources, relationships and external conditions change over the project lifecycle, so the sources of structural, socio-political, and emergent difficulty—and the needed management response—may also change.
