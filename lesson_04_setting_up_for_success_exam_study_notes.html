Overview, Guiding Principle & The Core Chain

Slides 01–06 • Learning Objectives, "Begin with the end in mind", Table 4.1 (Stakeholders → Requirements → Measures), Conflicting Needs & Delivering Value

Slide 02 Learning Objectives

Three Learning Objectives for Setting Up for Success

Objective 1
Stakeholders and success

Identify appropriate stakeholders and relevant success measures at the outset of a project and recognise their achievement or otherwise at completion.

Objective 2
Trade-offs

Demonstrate the need for and the implications of prioritising trade-offs.

Objective 3
Benefits and requirements

Recognise a structured approach to assessing the benefits of and requirements for a project.

🎓 Teaching Note (Prof. Naghdali Choupani) By the end of class, you should be able to identify whose requirements matter, define measures at different times, prioritise objectives, and trace expected benefits back to project changes.
Slide 03 Core Mindset

The guiding principle

📖 Exact Book Quote

"Begin with the end in mind."

🎓 Teaching Note A project can be called successful by one stakeholder and disappointing by another. Agree what outcome and value are sought before designing the work.
Slide 05 Conflicting Needs

Why one measure is rarely enough

📖 Exact Book Quote

"The challenge to achieving total success comes from the complexity of the objectives and the requirements of many different groups of stakeholders for the project, not least because there will be conflicts between the requirements of each."

⚠️ Teaching Note A project can satisfy a sponsor's budget target while harming users, staff or neighbours. List distinct groups and their requirements before deciding what "success" means.
Slide 06 Value vs. Output

Value is larger than the delivered product

📖 Exact Book Quote

"...a project is about delivering value rather than a product."

🎓 Teaching Note A finished building, service or system is an output. The value comes from what people can do with it and from how the work affects them. This distinction explains why stakeholder analysis belongs at project start.
Slide 04 • Table 4.1 Stakeholders, requirements and measures

The core chain: people → needs → evidence (Table 4.1)

1. Stakeholders (People) ➔ 2. Requirements (Needs) ➔ 3. Measures (Evidence)
Item Definition Examples
Stakeholders Any individual or group with an interest in the project process or outcome The project customers, the delivery team, the end beneficiaries of the project, anyone affected by the outcome, proponents and opponents of any change
Requirements What each individual or group wants from the process or outcome Defined requirements in the form of a specification or contract, implicit requirements (I want this project to be a good place to work), or legal/ethical/moral/commercial/competitive requirements
Measures The means by which it will be determined during and post project whether those requirements have been met Level of compliance to specification, customer satisfaction, level of return on investment, staff retention levels
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 04) Table 4.1 defines stakeholders, requirements and measures. A stakeholder has an interest in the process or outcome; a requirement expresses what they want; a measure lets the team check whether it was met.

Identify stakeholders beyond the formal team

1. Internal Project team and governance participants who do the work or make decisions.
2. External Customers, sponsors, beneficiaries, affected communities, regulators, suppliers and others outside the team.

Input/Process/Outcome Requirements, NPD Metrics Over Time & The 'How' of Delivery

Slide 10: Requirements refer to input, process or outcome (Table 4.2)

Dimension Input Process Outcome
Characteristics Limit on resources provided (quotes 1, 6) The environment in which the project is undertaken (quote 3); process resource consumption controlled (quote 4) Product meets certain requirements (quotes 1, 2, 5)
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 10): Input: limit the resources supplied. Process: shape how work is carried out. Outcome: specify what the product must do. Check all three; a team can meet an output specification while exhausting staff or damaging relationships.

Slide 11: Success measures change across time — Table 4.3 New product development (NPD) metrics

Type of measure Characteristic measured
Process measures Product development cost, time and conformance to quality procedures
Short-term outcome measures Product performance level, desirability to market, flexibility of design to be changed to meet emergent customer needs
Longer-term outcome measures Payback period, customer satisfaction, percentage of business being generated by the new product, market share, customisability for high-margin markets
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 11): The book contrasts process measures, short-term outcomes and longer-term outcomes. Decide who will measure each, and when.

Slide 12: How delivery feels also matters (The Restaurant Analogy)

📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 12)

"...the 'how' of project delivery, although less tangible, is highly significant in creating customer satisfaction."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 12) A project may meet time, budget and specification yet irritate clients or alienate stakeholders. The chapter compares this with a restaurant whose food is good but service is poor. Include process and relationship measures.
Slide 13 • Table 4.4 Conventional vs. Participatory M&E

Conventional and participatory evaluation (Table 4.4)

Dimension Conventional M&E Participatory M&E
Who plans and manages the process: Project managers Local people, project staff, managers and other stakeholders, often helped by a facilitator
Role of 'primary stakeholders' (intended beneficiaries): Provide information only Design and adapt the methodology, collect and analyse data, share findings and link them to action
How success is measured: Externally defined, mainly quantitative indicators Internally defined indicators, including more qualitative judgements
Approach: Pre-determined Adaptive
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 13) Conventional monitoring is mainly planned and managed by project managers. Participatory monitoring involves beneficiaries and other stakeholders in defining, gathering and using evidence, and adapts as needs emerge.

Slide 15 Slide 16
Worked Class Activity: Online Student-Enquiry Service

Activity: A student-services project & Suggested analysis: different end points

1. Teaching scenario A university proposes an online student-enquiry service. Identify a sponsor, student users, front-line staff and one less visible affected group.
2. Define success For three groups, write one requirement and one observable measure. Include a process or longer-term measure, not only launch date.
3. Engagement Place each group on a power-interest map and explain how you would involve them. What could make a group move quadrant?
Time Horizon 1
At launch

Service works, staff are prepared, accessibility and privacy needs are met.

Time Horizon 2
After a term

Students receive useful answers, repeat contacts fall, staff workload remains manageable.

Time Horizon 3
Over time

The university adapts the service using feedback and can show the value of the investment.

🎓 Teaching Notes (Slides 15 & 16)

• Stakeholder Values (Slide 15): Students may value accurate, timely answers; staff may value workable procedures and training; a sponsor may value affordability. Measure resolution quality as well as response time.

• Accountability (Slide 16): A single opening-date target misses later benefits and the experience of delivery. Put a named owner and assessment date against each measure.

Trade-Offs, Iron Triangle, Conformance vs. Performance & GTO

Slides 17–26 • Prioritising Objectives, Fig 4.4 ("Zone of no brain"), Figs 4.5–4.8 Priority Positions, Table 4.5 Conformance vs. Performance & Givens / Tradeables / Optimisers (GTO)

Slide 17 Definition of Trade-Off

Time, cost and quality create choices

📖 Exact Book Quote

"A trade-off is, therefore, the prioritisation of the objectives of a project."

🎓 Teaching Note The three objectives constrain one another. A shorter deadline can require more resources or a different specification. State priorities before planning, then use them when unexpected problems appear.
Slide 18 • Figure 4.4 Trade-offs in project management

The iron triangle & "Zone of no brain" (Figure 4.4)

🔍 How to Read It (Slide 18 – Exam Essential) The original triangle represents time, cost and quality performance. The central "zone of no brain" warns against claiming maximum performance on every dimension without real evidence of resources or choices.

📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 21: Do not leave priorities implicit)

"The project leader needs to ensure that the team are clear on the actual priorities if or when trade-offs need to be made, and that there is a common, shared, view."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 21) If different team members optimise different objectives, work can pull in incompatible directions. Record who agreed the priority and what decision it will guide when conditions change.
Slide 22 • Table 4.5 Conformance versus performance

Conformance versus performance: attributes of time, cost and quality (Table 4.5)

Dimension Time Cost Quality
Performance Shortest possible Cheapest possible Highest level
Conformance As planned As budgeted As specified
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 22) Conformance asks whether the project met its stated time, cost and quality targets (as planned, as budgeted, as specified). Performance pushes for the shortest duration, lowest cost or highest quality. A project can conform to a weak target without excelling.
Slide 23 Limits of the Iron Triangle

Beyond time, cost and quality

📖 Exact Book Quote

"time, cost and quality are incomplete as a statement of requirements for a project."

🎓 Teaching Note Legal, ethical, safety, environmental, service, staff and stakeholder benefits may matter. A project also needs room to handle requirements that become clearer during delivery.
Slide 24 • GTO Framework Slide 25 Slide 26
Givens, Tradeables & Optimisers (GTO)

Slide 24: GTO – Name the status of every objective

G = Givens (Non-Negotiable)

Objectives that must be achieved and cannot be traded, such as compliance with relevant law or a necessary safety standard.

T = Tradeables (Flexible)

Desirable objectives that can be adjusted to protect more important aims, such as optional features or a flexible scope element.

O = Optimisers (Maximise)

Outcomes stakeholders want more of, such as citizen benefit or less disruption, pursued without violating givens.

⚠️ Teaching Note (Slide 24 – Why Tradeables Are Essential) GTO means givens, tradeables and optimisers. It widens the discussion beyond the iron triangle. A workable plan needs genuine tradeables; otherwise the apparent agreement hides an impossible promise.

Slides 25 & 26 Activity: Negotiate a campus opening & Suggested GTO decision

Slide 25: Scenario & Contractor Dilemma

• Scenario: A campus must be ready for a September intake. The conversion budget is tight; an optional social area, accessibility, fire safety and staff training remain unresolved.

• Choose: Classify opening date, legal safety, accessibility, training, optional social space and cost as givens, tradeables or optimisers.

• Respond: A contractor offers to save two weeks by postponing accessibility work. Decide using your classification and propose a safer adjustment.

Suggested Classification: Legal safety and accessibility are givens; optional social space is a possible tradeable. The deadline may be a given if teaching cannot be rescheduled. Training should not be removed without checking operational readiness.

Slide 26: Suggested GTO Decision

1. Reject the shortcut: Do not trade away required safety or accessibility for time.

2. Protect the real date: Keep the September opening as a given only if feasible with critical requirements met.

3. Adjust what can move: Phase optional facilities, add suitable capacity, revise noncritical scope, or escalate a budget change with evidence.

Teaching Note: The classification provides a reasoned decision rule. The sponsor may need to decide whether cost or scope can change; the team should disclose the implications rather than quietly weakening standards.

Delivering Value, Balanced Scorecard & Benefits Mapping

Slides 27–33 • Yielding Value, 4 Balanced Scorecard Perspectives, Fig 4.9 Benefits Map for PPM Capability, Student Enquiries Map & Testing Intangible Benefits (Pilot, Benchmark, Model)

Slide 27 Core Purpose of Projects

A project should yield value

📖 Exact Book Quote

"The fundamental idea is that a project should yield value: value to someone at some point in time."

🎓 Teaching Note Value may be financial, social or experiential. Specify who benefits and when; "the project is finished" is not itself a benefit.
Slide 28 Balanced Scorecard Framework

Four perspectives of the balanced scorecard

1. Financial

What financial benefit will the project create for owners or funders?

2. Customers and suppliers

What direct benefit, goodwill or new relationship will they gain?

3. Internal processes

Which organisational process will work better?

4. Innovation and learning

What knowledge or capability will the work create?

Slide 33 Activity: Trace a Benefit All the Way Back

• Task: Choose a campus benefit (e.g., employer partnerships or student access). Write 1 required organisational change + 1 enabling action, plus 1 launch measure & 1 later measure.

• Suggested Test: If a new facility opens but the intended benefit does not appear, which assumption in the chain would you examine first?

➔ Suggested Answer: Opening a building does not automatically create partnerships. Check whether programmes, agreements, staffing and employer engagement changed, then examine whether the benefit measure was realistic.

Slide 31 Slide 32
Pilot, Benchmark & Model

Make intangible benefits more testable & Worked example

📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 31)

"carrying out a pilot study — determine on a small scale the costs and benefits of the work being proposed before launching into a full-scale project;"

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 31): Alongside a pilot study, the book also proposes benchmarking/reference sites and modelling. State what evidence each method could produce and what uncertainty remains.
1. Pilot (Slide 32): Trial the service with one department for six weeks; record waiting time, resolution quality and staff hours.
2. Benchmark (Slide 32): Compare with a similar service, noting differences in student numbers and processes.
3. Model (Slide 32): Estimate full rollout demand using the pilot and test low, expected and high volumes.
⚠️ Teaching Note (Slide 32): A model is only as reliable as its inputs; document assumptions and who will own later benefits.

Book Case Study: A New Campus for the University of Rummidge

Slides 34–38 • Rummidge/Splot Scenario (€25m, 12 Months), Fig 4.11 University Governance Structure & Case Questions 1, 2 & 3

Slide 34 Slide 35 • Figure 4.11
University of Rummidge / Splot Case & Governance Structure

Slide 34: Book case – A new campus for Rummidge

1. Opportunity The university cannot expand easily at its existing site; the town (Splot) seeks prestige and higher education provision.
2. Proposal Convert former school premises for courses the following September; suggested budget €25 million.
3. Challenge The team has 12 months, conditional funding, staff concerns, travel distance and uncertainty about local support.

Slide 35: University governance affects the project (Figure 4.11 Outline structure – University of Rummidge)

1. Senate Election & Turnover: "The senate are elected by the staff of the university, with 25 per cent of members being required to stand down each year and be replaced."
2. Heads of Academic Departments: "Heads of academic departments are nominated from the Professoriate of their department, but with little direct control over the academic staff."
3. Heads of Administrative Departments: "Heads of administrative departments are functional specialists in their particular area, e.g. finance."
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 35) The book's outline shows a vice-chancellor, senate and academic and administrative departments. Academic staff had limited input to negotiations. Identify whose agreement is required and who can make operational changes.
Slide 36 • Case Question 1

Question 1: Objectives and benefits map

"What are the strategic objectives for each of the organisations involved? Provide a high-level benefits map to structure the objectives and necessary changes."

University: List the strategic benefit it seeks, one organisational change and one enabling action.
Town council: Do the same. Where do objectives align, and where could they conflict?
🎓 Suggested Analysis (Slide 36) The university seeks capacity and income beyond its current site; the town seeks education provision, redevelopment and prestige. Both need viable courses, staffing, facilities and funding.
Slide 37 • Case Question 2

Question 2: Stakeholder strategy

"Who are the key stakeholders in such a project? Show how each group should be managed."

Identify: Include students, staff, university leadership, senate, town council, local residents, employers, funders and regulators.
Engage: For three groups, state their need, potential influence and an appropriate way to involve them.
🎓 Suggested Analysis (Slide 37) Academic and administrative staff require early dialogue on workload and course design; students need clear information and usable services; funders need evidence of viability. Update the map as positions change.
Slide 38 • Case Question 3

Question 3: What would success look like?

"What would appropriate success measures be for the project?"

1. At opening: Safe, accessible, equipped facilities; approved courses; prepared staff and support services.
2. After one year: Student demand, course quality, employer participation, staff workload and financial performance.
3. Longer term: Sustainable provision, regional contribution and value for both university and town.
🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 38) Suggested measures should be defined with target, data source, owner and review date.
