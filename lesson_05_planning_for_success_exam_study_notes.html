🎓 Teaching Note (Prof. Naghdali Choupani) By the end, you should be able to outline a project before detailed scheduling, decide how much planning is useful, and use stages and gates to test whether work should continue.
Slide 03 D1 "Define it" Stage

The plan should serve the project

📖 Exact Book Quote

"The approach to planning determines the nature, role and ultimately the utility of the plan."

🎓 Teaching Note Planning choices shape what the plan can do: explore an idea, test feasibility, coordinate work and support decisions. Chapter 5 is the D1 "Define it" stage; detailed time scheduling follows in Chapter 6.
Slide 04 • Figure 5.1 Initial planning

The five elements of an initial plan (Figure 5.1)

1. Product What outcome/output is being delivered.
2. Process Delivery process & stages/gates used.
3. Tasks Constituent activities required.
4. People / organisation Team, roles & stakeholders involved.
5. Costs Budget, resources & financial constraints connected to the plan.
🔍 How to Read It The initial plan connects product, delivery process, tasks, people/organisation and costs. It is an outline that makes detailed planning possible and lets a sponsor decide whether further work is justified.

Creativity, Idea Funnel & Concept Screening

Slides 05–09 • Order & Chaos, Concept Development Funnel, Screening Filters, Route to Reality & Skunk Works

Slide 05 Slide 06 • Concept Funnel
Divergent Exploration → Convergent Screening

Creativity and order both have a place & Ideas expand, then pass through filters

📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 05)

"...they are characterised by a combination of order and chaos"

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 05) Early idea generation can be broad; later planning narrows and organises. Eliminating exploration too soon may miss good alternatives, while never converging leaves an unmanageable project.
Slide 06 Diagram: Concept Development & Product Delivery Funnel
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 06) Inputs include staff, customers, suppliers, competitors and R&D. The number of ideas first rises, then falls as concepts are screened. Some ideas become products, licenses or new businesses; others are recycled or rejected.
Slide 07 Early Screening Filters

Screen ideas before committing resources

1. Student dissertation filters: Interest, feasibility with available time/resources, and enough theory to support the study.
2. New product filters: Market appeal, financial return, fit with mission and technical possibility.
3. Decision rule: Keep evidence and assumptions visible; a promising idea can still fail a feasibility or strategy test.
🎓 Teaching Note: Screening is an early gate, not a verdict that every rejected idea lacks value.
Slide 08 Creative Concept Development

Give good ideas a route to reality

1. Explore and test: Allow time to investigate; prototype early and rehearse how an idea works.
2. Support: Protect idea ownership and identify a senior champion who can seek resources.
3. Develop: Use clear criteria and a rapid development route for ideas with potential.
🎓 Teaching Note: The book lists these as common features of creative concept development. A process can preserve creativity when it makes testing and progression possible.
Slide 09 Historical Lockheed Case

Skunk Works: freedom with backing

📖 Exact Book Quote

"Its experts were given the freedom to try completely new ideas and they achieved huge successes."

Teaching Context (Lockheed Example) Pairs a small team's freedom to try new technologies with support from a larger organisation. Ask what constraints should remain visible even when bureaucracy is reduced.
🎓 Teaching Point: Autonomy helps exploration, while funding, technical capability and review are still needed to decide what progresses.

Managing Scope & Change

Slides 10–16 • Product vs. Process Scope, Sinclair C5 Scope Creep (Fig 5.3), Freeze Point, Late Change Dilemma & Career Fair Scope Statement

Slide 10 Definition of Scope

Scope describes the project output

📖 Exact Book Quote

"The product of the project, the outcome or output, is described as 'the scope'."

🎓 Teaching Note Scope should say what will be delivered and what will be excluded. The chapter also stresses process scope: how work and stakeholder commitments will be handled. A change can improve value but has time and cost consequences.
Slide 11 • Figure 5.3 Scope creep

The Sinclair C5: scope can drift (Figure 5.3)

🔍 How to Read It The book contrasts an initial four-seat electric-vehicle concept with the final electrically assisted tricycle. Repeated scope changes altered the product and it was not accepted by consumers. Treat it as a historical caution, not proof that all change is bad.
Slide 12 Product Scope, Process Scope & Freeze Point

Product scope and process scope both need agreement

1. Product scope: What the outcome must do; its boundaries, requirements and exclusions.
2. Process scope: How the work will be done, including access, decisions, reviews and stakeholder contributions.
3. Freeze point: Agree when a version of scope becomes the planning basis and how later changes will be considered.
⚠️ Teaching Note (Baseline Changes) The book explicitly distinguishes product and process scope. A frozen baseline can be changed, but not silently: assess expected benefit, cost, time and stakeholder effects first.
Slide 13 Change Control Dilemma

Activity: would you accept a late change?

Book scenario: An oil-and-gas plant refurbishment is ready for detailed design when an improved technology appears. It may save substantial operating cost but delays the project.
Decision: Identify information needed before accepting or rejecting the change: likely savings, delay, re-engineering cost, safety and stakeholder effects.
Record: State the decision and update needed in scope, estimate and approval record.
🎓 Teaching Note The book poses this dilemma without a single automatic answer. A good response compares the incremental lifetime value with delay and change cost, and secures a conscious decision by the right authority.
Slide 14 Slide 15 Slide 16
Written Scope Agreement & Career-Fair Scope Exercise

Write down and agree the scope & Career-fair scope statement

📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 14)

"...if you don't produce a clear statement of scope that can be discussed and agreed by the key stakeholders, one will almost certainly emerge that will not be what you thought it would be."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 14 – Sponsored Student-Project Case) In the book's sponsored student-project case, verbal promises and missing access to people/data led to conflicting expectations. Record deliverables, support needed, acceptance criteria and responsibilities, then obtain stakeholder agreement.
Slide 15 Prompt (Draft a One-Page Scope Statement) Slide 16 Suggested Scope Response (Career Fair)
Teaching scenario: A student team plans a one-day career fair. The aim is employer contact and useful information for students, within a modest budget. Deliverable: One-day fair with booked employers, accessible venue, schedule and student information.
Write (4 min): Define the product, three inclusions, two exclusions, one acceptance criterion and the stakeholder who signs off. Boundary: Include registration and event communications; exclude entertainment and ongoing placement services.
Acceptance: Sponsor approves scope; venue and safety requirements are met; agreed employer participation is confirmed.
Challenge (3 min): A sponsor asks for an evening concert two weeks before the event. What changes to scope, cost, safety, staffing and approvals would you assess? Late Change Response: A late concert should be assessed as a new request, not assumed to be included. Ask whether it supports the fair's benefit and whether resources and approvals can safely accommodate it.

Planning Balance, Uncertainty & Planning Stances

Slides 17–23 • Fig 5.4 Costs vs. Benefits, Working Tool, Product/Process Uncertainty, 4 Planning Stances, Fig 5.5 Rolling Wave, Agile Sprints & Accuracy vs. Precision

Slide 17 • Figure 5.4 Slide 18
Balancing Costs and Benefits of Planning

Planning effort should earn its cost (Figure 5.4) & A plan is a working tool

❌ Misuse of Planning Costs Outweigh Benefits
  • • Plan as a straitjacket (rigid defence of an obsolete course)
  • • Focus on precision (false decimal places over weak assumptions)
  • • Generation of data (volume of charts without decision value)
✅ Good Use of Planning Benefits Outweigh Costs
  • • Plan as a working tool (helps decision-making & guides action)
  • • Focus on accuracy (testing fundamental soundness first)
  • • Provision of information (avoiding disorder, finding problems early)
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 17) Planning uses labour, tools, document preparation and opportunity cost. Its benefits include avoiding disorder, evaluating ideas and finding problems early. The goal is useful information, not the largest possible plan.
📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 18)

"As a working tool, plans are used to help decision-making and guide future activities."

🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 18) Update the plan when circumstances change. A plan should support decisions, not become a rigid defence of an obsolete course of action. The objectives remain the reason for planning.
Slide 19 Sources of Uncertainty

Uncertainty can be in the product or process

1. Product uncertainty: Requirements or benefits change while the output is being developed (e.g., IT requirements change).
2. Process uncertainty: Work is disrupted by discoveries, resources or team interactions (e.g., unexpected archaeological discovery).
3. Planning implication: State assumptions and choose a plan with appropriate review and contingency.
🎓 Teaching Note: Both product and process uncertainty can affect sequence, duration and resources.
Slide 20 Selecting a Planning Approach

Four broad planning stances

1. Claim to know everything Works best for genuinely repeatable work; challenge optimism bias.
2. Claim to know nothing Milestones without a credible route can become wishful thinking.
3. Rolling wave Plan near work in detail while keeping later work at overview level.
4. Agile sprints Fix near-term outputs for a short sprint, keep a backlog, then integrate results.
🎓 Teaching Note These are the chapter's four categories. Select by evidence about uncertainty and repeatability, not by a fashionable label. Agile short cycles still require integration at project level.
Slide 21 • Figure 5.5 Slide 22 Slide 23
Rolling-Wave Detail, Agile Sprints & Accuracy Before False Precision

Slide 21: Rolling-wave detail grows over time (Figure 5.5)

🔍 How to Read It (Slide 21): The graph shows detailed planning increasing as each phase approaches. Set the planning horizon to the pace of work, resource lead-times and next milestone. Keep the overall purpose and major dates visible throughout.
Slide 22 • Agile Sprints

Agile work is planned in short cycles

"...an agile project is broken down into short sprints lasting a number of weeks (1–6 is common)"

Teaching Note: At the start of each sprint, define intended outputs; unfinished items go to a backlog. The chapter warns that outputs from separate sprints still need integration, especially in a complex project.

Slide 23 • Accuracy vs. Precision

Accuracy before false precision

"would you rather be roughly right or precisely wrong?"

Teaching Note: Test the overview for fundamental technical or financial flaws before spending time on detailed estimates. More decimal places do not cure weak assumptions. Revise the outline before refining details.

The Planning Process, ICOM Model & PID

Slides 24–29 • Fig 5.6 ICOM Model, Tactical "What" vs. Operational "How", Fig 5.7 Planning Sequence, Brief vs. PID vs. Charter & 12 PRINCE2 PID Contents

Slide 24 • Figure 5.6 Activity model using ICOMs (Inputs, Controls, Outputs, Mechanisms)

The planning process has inputs and controls (Figure 5.6)

🔍 How to Read It (Slide 24) The ICOM model shows the brief as input, proposal as output, standards/constraints as controls, and people/tools as mechanisms. Use it to ask what the planning team needs, what limits it, and what it must produce.
Slide 25 Tactical vs. Operational

Tactical "what" becomes operational "how"

📖 Exact Book Quote

"At one level it has to be decided 'what' happens. This, the tactical-level plan, then needs to be converted into a statement of 'how' it is going to be carried out (or operationalised) at the operational level."

🎓 Teaching Note First agree the major work and intended result. Then decide sequence, resources, roles and methods. The proposal should go through substantial revisions before small refinements.
Slide 26 • Figure 5.7 The project planning process

A simple planning sequence (Figure 5.7)

🔍 How to Read It (1) Identify constituent activities, (2) determine logical sequence, (3) estimate time/resources, and (4) present the plan clearly. The figure is an overview; detailed scheduling techniques arrive in Chapter 6.
Slide 27 Slide 28 Slide 29
Brief vs. PID vs. Charter & The 12 PRINCE2 PID Contents

Slide 27: Brief, PID (Project Initiation Document) and charter serve different roles

1. Brief (Request)

The client's or sponsor's initial request or terms of reference.

2. PID / Proposal (Response)

The project team's outline response: scope, approach, justification, roles, plan and controls.

3. Charter (Authorisation)

The sponsor's formal authorisation to start and use organisational resources.

Slide 27 Teaching Note: The book contrasts a PID prepared in answer to a brief with a charter that officially authorises the project. The formality should match complexity, organisational practice and audience.

Slide 28: What belongs in the PID? (12 PRINCE2 PID Contents Grouped)

1. Purpose and value Definition, desired outcomes, scope/exclusions, constraints, approach and business case.
2. People and standards Management structure, role descriptions, quality and communication approaches.
3. Controls and delivery Change and risk approaches, main plan, stage boundaries, reporting and tailoring.
🎓 Teaching Note (Slide 28): This groups the twelve PRINCE2 PID contents listed in the book. Add the stakeholder interests and performance objectives the chapter recommends.

Slide 29 Activity: Turn a brief into a proposal outline

Brief: A sponsor requests the student career fair. No venue, employers or funding have yet been confirmed.
Prepare: Write a 1-min executive summary, assumptions to verify, main deliverables, a rough sequence and one gate before committing the venue.
Review & Suggested Response: Confirm demand, sponsor budget, accessible venue and employer interest before detailed bookings. The proposal shows benefits, scope, risk and decision points; the charter then authorises work.

Stage-Gate Model & Concurrent Engineering

Slides 30–35 • Fig 5.8 Stage-Gate Model, 7 Reasons to Halt, Table 5.1 Gate Questions, Fig 5.10 Miscommunication & Fig 5.12 Sequential vs. Concurrent Work

Slide 30 • Figure 5.8 Slide 31
Stage-Gate Model (After Cooper, 1988) & Reasons for Calling a Halt

Stages create natural review points (Figure 5.8) & Stopping can be the responsible decision

🔍 How to Read It (Slide 30) A stage-gate plan separates work into phases with review decisions between them. Set pass criteria before work begins. Each gate asks whether the next commitment is still justified, not only whether the previous work finished.

Slide 31: Stopping can be the responsible decision (7 Reasons Grouped into 3 Categories)

1. Value changed Most benefit is already obtained, or a better alternative has appeared.
2. Assumptions failed Estimates are badly wrong, needed capability is absent or key people leave.
3. Strategic or financial fit Strategy changes, or continuing would endanger the organisation financially.
⚠️ Teaching Note & Sunk-Cost Warning (Slide 31) These group the chapter's seven reasons for calling a halt. A gate may also lead to re-scope, partnership or licensing rather than immediate cancellation. Past expenditure alone does not justify more spending.

Slide 35 • Design a First Gate

• Set criteria: For the career fair, require sponsor budget, employer interest, venue feasibility and a basic safety/accessibility review.

• Decide (Go / Revise / Stop): Hold concept until essential feasibility is shown; revise if venue/date can change; stop if no affordable, safe arrangement exists. A gate protects resources and provides a deliberate choice.

Slide 42 Case Evaluation Activity

Activity: critique the Mini proposal

1. Find one strength Use evidence from the PID, WBS, four-fields map or Gantt chart.
➔ Clear testable objectives, explicit Gate 1 criteria, and assigned team responsibilities are strengths.
2. Find one gap Identify an assumption, stakeholder, scope boundary, dependency or safety requirement that needs stronger evidence.
➔ Permissions, insurance liability coverage, participant interest and safe crowding capacity need stronger verification.
3. Decide at Gate 1 State go, revise or stop, and the evidence required before the next commitment.
➔ A cautious reviewer would verify permissions, written insurance, participant interest and safe capacity before approving the event.

Exit Check: Plan Before Scheduling

Slide 43 • Final Exam Self-Test & Complete Suggested Answers

Slide 43 • Final Review Three High-Yield Exam Questions
1. Name the five elements of an initial plan and explain why an outline comes before detailed scheduling.

➔ Suggested Answer: The five elements are product, process, tasks, people/organisation and costs. An initial outline comes before detailed scheduling so the team and sponsor can test fundamental feasibility, scope and value before spending effort on detailed estimates ("roughly right before precisely wrong").

2. Distinguish a brief, PID and charter in one sentence each.

➔ Suggested Answer: • Brief: The client's or sponsor's initial request or terms of reference.
• PID (Project Initiation Document): The project team's outline proposal and response setting out scope, justification, approach, roles, plan and controls.
• Charter: The sponsor's formal authorisation to start the project and commit organisational resources.

3. Give one gate criterion and one reason a project might be revised or stopped.

➔ Suggested Answer: • Gate criterion: Confirming viability before committing more resources (e.g., confirmed sponsor budget, university insurance coverage, health & safety consent, or suitable venue/car availability).
• Reason to revise or stop: Value changed (better alternative appeared / benefit already obtained), assumptions failed (estimates badly wrong, missing capability, key people leave), or loss of strategic/financial fit (continuing endangers the organisation).
