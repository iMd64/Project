Slide 03 Core Distinction: Plan vs. Schedule vs. Gantt Chart

A time plan is an analysis tool

📖 Exact Book Quote

"Having prepared a plan (not date dependent), the next stage is to compile the schedule (e.g. we will start on this date). The Gantt chart is just one way to display this all-important schedule."

🎓 Teaching Note First establish what work is needed, its sequence and estimated duration. Then apply start dates, calendars and available resources to make a schedule. A Gantt chart displays that schedule.
Visualizing the 3-Stage Progression (Exam Essential)
STAGE 1: TIME PLAN

Not Date Dependent

  • What work is needed (WBS)
  • Sequence / logic (Network)
  • Estimated duration (e.g., Day 0 to Day 24)
STAGE 2: SCHEDULE

Date & Resource Dependent

  • Apply specific start dates ("start on this date")
  • Apply working calendars (weekends/holidays)
  • Apply available resources & capacity
STAGE 3: GANTT CHART

Display Tool

  • Just one visual way to display the schedule
  • Places activity bars on a horizontal time axis
  • For others to read, challenge, and use

Breaking the Project Down (PBS, WBS, OBS)

Slides 04–11 • Core Planning Elements, Deconstruction, WBS, Elephant Analogy, OBS, PBS & Campus Event Activity

Slide 04 • Figure 6.1 Core planning elements

Five linked elements of detailed planning (Figure 6.1)

Core Element Breakdown / Map
Product Product breakdown structure (PBS)
Tasks Work breakdown structure (WBS)
People / organisation Organisational breakdown structure (OBS)
Process Detailed process map
Costs Cost breakdown structure
🔍 How to Read It Product → product breakdown; tasks → work breakdown; people → organisational breakdown. Process maps and cost breakdowns complete the picture. A change in one element may force revisions in the others.
Slide 05 Why We Deconstruct a Project

Deconstruction must support two purposes

📖 Exact Book Quote

"First, it must provide an opportunity for the planner to analyse the project system, to reveal opportunities for improvement and problem prevention, and setting out the basis on which the project will proceed."

🎓 Teaching Note Breaking work down helps reveal improvements and problems before execution. The result also has to be communicable so others can challenge and use it. A detailed chart with no decision purpose adds little value.
Slide 06 • Figure 6.2 Example of a work breakdown structure (WBS)

A WBS (Work Breakdown Structure) turns the whole into manageable work

🔍 How to Read It Read the computer-system WBS from the whole project to lower-level activities. The lowest useful level can be assigned, estimated and monitored. Notice that activities are phrased as actions, such as select, trial and implement.
Slide 07 Key WBS Limitation & Handoffs

Work packages still have to fit together (The Elephant Analogy)

📖 Exact Book Quote

"...the main challenge is to join all of these pieces up. Returning to the elephant analogy, the challenge is not about eating the elephant, but how do you join all of the pieces up to make a working elephant. This is a much trickier proposition."

⚠️ Teaching Note & Exam Takeaway: Managing Interfaces A WBS can make each part manageable but obscure handoffs. For every interface between tasks, you must identify three things:
  1. Who owns each interface
  2. What information passes across it
  3. How completion will be checked before the next task begins
Slide 08 • Figure 6.3 OBS = "Who?"

The OBS (Organization Breakdown Structure) identifies the people needed

🔍 How to Read It The example separates finance, IT and contractors, then names roles within them. The OBS identifies potential human resources; it does not yet allocate each person to every task or prove their availability.
Slide 09 • Figure 6.4 PBS = "What will be delivered?"

The PBS (Product/Project Breakdown Structure) identifies the parts of the output

🔍 How to Read It The computer system divides into hardware and software, then components such as servers, cabling, main package and security. PBS asks "what will be delivered?" WBS asks "what work must be done?"

Estimating Task Durations

Slides 12–14 • Table 6.1 Estimate Types, Building Useful Estimates & Evaluating Claims

Slide 12 • Table 6.1 The nature, role and accuracy of estimate types

Four estimate types have different uses (Table 6.1)

Name Nature Role Accuracy
Rough / finger-in-the-air / ballpark Much uncertainty as to what is involved Early check on feasibility of brief Very low
As-buts As was carried out previously, but with the following amendments – some quantitative data exists With an appropriate contingency factor – can be used for proposals Moderate
Detailed estimates Some initial work is carried out to determine what the likely problems are going to be Proposals Moderate
… to finish Much of the project is completed and additional funding is needed to complete the tasks Additional funds request High
🔍 How to Read It The book ranges from rough feasibility estimates (very low accuracy), through adjusted historical ("as-buts") and detailed proposal estimates (moderate), to "to finish" estimates near completion (high). Accuracy grows as work and evidence accumulate.
Slide 13 Task-Duration Estimation

Build a useful task-duration estimate

1. Evidence: Ask what happened on comparable work and what differs here.
2. Conditions: State people, working calendar, approval delays and likely pitfalls.
3. Revision: Re-estimate when work or assumptions change; keep the old basis for comparison.
🎓 Teaching Note (Duration vs. Effort) The chapter stresses previous experience, competing work and potential delays. A duration is elapsed task time under assumptions, not simply effort hours or a guarantee.
Slide 14 Concept Check

Check: three estimates, three claims

1. Rough Claim:

"The course redesign might take about 6 weeks." — What evidence is missing?

➔ Answer: Lacks scope and task data.

2. Analogous ("As-buts") Claim:

"A similar course took 8 weeks; we need a new assessment." — What adjustment is needed?

➔ Answer: Requires adjustment for assessment design.

3. Detailed Claim:

"Named staff estimated each task and the handoffs." — What still needs checking?

➔ Answer: Still needs dependencies, resource availability, calendars and uncertainty.

⚠️ Golden Rule of Estimates Never label a number more precise than its evidence supports.

Network Logic (Activity-on-Node / AoN)

Slides 15–20 • Three Inputs, Simple Dependency, 4 Linkages (FS, SS, FF, SF), Merge/Burst & 6-Number Box Notation

Slide 15 Inputs to a Network

Activity-on-node combines three inputs

1. Activities

Take the work packages from the WBS.

2. Logic

State which activities must precede others.

3. Duration

Estimate the time each activity will take.

🎓 Teaching Note Each box represents an activity; arrows represent precedence. The network lets the class test the proposed sequence before placing dates on a calendar.
Slide 16 • Figure 6.5 Task dependency

A simple dependency: 6 + 7 = 13 days

🔍 How to Read It Activity B cannot start until A finishes. With A = 6 days and B = 7 days, the earliest completion is day 13. This is a finish-to-start dependency, with day 0 as the starting point.
Slide 17 • Figure 6.6 Logical activity linkages

Four ways to link two activities (Figure 6.6)

1. Finish-to-start (FS) Default

B starts after A finishes. (FS is used in the worked network).

2. Start-to-start (SS)

B starts after A starts. (Arrow leaves start of A → enters start of B).

3. Finish-to-finish (FF)

B finishes after A finishes. (Arrow leaves finish of A → enters finish of B).

4. Start-to-finish (SF)

B finishes after A starts. (Arrow leaves start of A → enters finish of B).

Slide 18 • Figure 6.7 Multiple dependencies (1)

One successor may have two prerequisites

ES(C) = max[ EF(A), EF(B) ]
🔍 How to Read It C waits for both A and B. Its earliest start is the later of their earliest finish times: ES(C) = max[EF(A), EF(B)]. A delay in either predecessor matters only if it makes that predecessor the latest to finish.
Slide 19 • Figure 6.8 Multiple dependencies (2)

Branches must rejoin before shared work

🔍 How to Read It The final activity H waits for E, F and G. Parallel work may reduce overall duration, but the latest finishing predecessor controls H's start. List every true dependency; do not link unrelated tasks for convenience.
Slide 20 • Figure 6.10 Activity notation

The activity box holds six numbers (Figure 6.10)

🔍 Key Definitions in the Box
  • • ES / EST: Earliest start time (top-left)
  • • Dur: Duration (occupies top-centre)
  • • EF / EFT: Earliest finish time (top-right)
  • • Label: Activity identifier/name (middle row)
  • • LS / LST: Latest start time (bottom-left)
  • • Float: Allowable delay without changing project finish (bottom-centre)
  • • LF / LFT: Latest finish time (bottom-right)

Forward Pass & Reverse Pass

Slides 21–26 • Left-to-Right Earliest Times (Fig 6.11–6.12) & Right-to-Left Latest Times (Fig 6.13)

Slide 21 Slide 22 • Fig 6.11 Slide 23 Slide 24 • Fig 6.12
Worked Forward Pass (Left → Right)

Forward pass: earliest feasible times & the book's 8-activity network

Rule 1: Start

Set the first activity ES = 0; then EF = ES + duration. (Uses day 0, not a calendar date).

Rule 2: Single Predecessor

ES of a successor = predecessor EF.

Rule 3: Several Predecessors

ES = maximum EF among all immediate predecessors.

Top Path
A – B – E – H
5 + 3 + 6 + 8 = 22 days
Middle Path (Longest / Candidate Critical Path)
A – C – F – H
5 + 4 + 7 + 8 = 24 days
Bottom Path
A – D – G – H
5 + 5 + 5 + 8 = 23 days
ES(H) = max[ EF(E)=14, EF(F)=16, EF(G)=15 ] = 16  ➔  EF(H) = 16 + 8 = 24 days
Figure 6.11 (Durations) + Figure 6.12 (Results from the Forward Pass – Top Row Completed)
🔍 How to Read It (Slides 21–24) Work from left to right through the network. A finishes day 5. B/C/D finish day 8/9/10; E/F/G finish day 14/16/15. H can begin only at day 16 ("Latest of the possible ESTs"). Early times: A: 0–5; B: 5–8; C: 5–9; D: 5–10; E: 8–14; F: 9–16; G: 10–15; H: 16–24. The project duration is 24 days.
Slide 25 Slide 26 • Figure 6.13
Worked Reverse Pass (Right → Left)

Reverse pass: latest permissible times (Figure 6.13)

Rule 1: Finish

Set the last activity LF = project duration (24) ("Make EFT = LFT at finish activity"); LS = LF − duration.

Rule 2: Single Successor

LF of a predecessor = successor LS.

Rule 3: Several Successors

LF = minimum LS among immediate successors ("Earliest of the possible LFTs").

Figure 6.13 (Results of the Forward and Reverse Passes)
🔍 How to Read It (Slides 25–26) Work right to left. Latest times mean the activity can move that far without delaying the 24-day finish under the current network. H has LS/LF 16/24. E, F and G have LS/LF 10/16, 9/16 and 11/16. B, C and D have 7/10, 5/9 and 6/11. At A, since B, C, and D have LS of 7, 5, and 6, A takes the minimum (earliest of the possible LFTs) = 5, so A remains 0/5.

Float, Critical Path & Duration Changes

Slides 27–29 • Total Float Formula, Shared Float Trap, Completed Network (Fig 6.14) & Duration Variation

Slide 27 Slide 28 • Figure 6.14
Total Float & Highlighted Critical Path

Float identifies flexibility & The critical path is A–C–F–H

Total float = LS − ES = LF − EF
0 Days Float (Critical Path)
Activities A, C, F, H

Sum of durations: 5 + 4 + 7 + 8 = 24 days.

1 Day Float
Activities D, G

D: 6 − 5 = 1  |  G: 11 − 10 = 1

2 Days Float
Activities B, E

B: 7 − 5 = 2  |  E: 10 − 8 = 2

Figure 6.14 Completed network analysis including floats and highlighted critical path (A–C–F–H)
⚠️ Exam Trap: Shared Float (Slide 27) Activities with zero total float lie on the current critical path. Noncritical float is shared along a path: do not spend a day of D and a day of G as though they were independent buffers. (If D is delayed by 1 day, G starts on day 11 and has 0 float left!).
🔍 How to Read It (Slide 28) Its durations sum to 5 + 4 + 7 + 8 = 24 days. Any delay on this path delays the modelled finish unless offset elsewhere. The path is a management focus, not proof that noncritical tasks need no attention.
Slide 29 Dynamic Criticality

Change the durations; recheck the path (Book Variation)

1. Book Variation

C is completed one day early (4 → 3 days), while D takes two extra days (5 → 7 days).

2. Recalculate

A–C–F–H becomes 23 days (5+3+7+8);
A–D–G–H becomes 25 days (5+7+5+8).

3. Conclusion

The critical path moves to A–D–G–H; project duration becomes 25 days.

🎓 Teaching Note The book's variation shows that criticality is not a permanent property of a task. Recompute after material duration or dependency changes.

From Plan to Schedule, Gantt Charts & Software

Slides 30–38 • Fig 6.16 Schedule Development, Calendars, Gantt Bars, Fig 6.18 Dissertation Plan, Forward/Backward Scheduling & "Garbage in, Gospel out"

Slide 30 • Figure 6.16 Schedule development process

Plan first, schedule second (Figure 6.16)

🔍 How to Read It The chapter combines project logic, estimates and resource capability/capacity to create a schedule. A plan can show relative days; a schedule specifies dates and who can do the work.
Slide 31 Working Days vs. Calendar Days

Calendars change real dates

1. Working time: Weekends, holidays and working hours change elapsed dates.
2. Resource capacity: A person or machine cannot do unlimited simultaneous work.
3. Dependency dates: Waiting for a predecessor or approval may push the next task.
⚠️ Teaching Note A 24-day network is 24 modelled working days, not necessarily 24 calendar days. Check resource availability before accepting a software-generated finish date.

Slide 34: Schedule from either end

1. Forward Scheduling:

From an available start, when could each task and the project finish?

2. Backward Scheduling:

From a required finish (e.g., hand-in date), when is the latest each task can start?

3. Compare:

Check whether the deadline is feasible, then consider resources and contingency.

🎓 Teaching Note: Both use task logic and duration. A backward schedule may reveal that a desired date requires earlier work, parallel activity or a scope change.
Slide 35 Slide 36
Gantt Strengths, Limits & Risk

Gantt charts: useful, with limits & "Garbage in, Gospel out"

Strengths Simple to draw/read; good overview; widely used; software-friendly.
Limits Manual updates can be hard; time bars do not show cost or optimise resources.
Risk A neat chart may imply false certainty even when inputs are weak.
📖 Exact Book Quote (Slide 36)

"'Garbage in, Gospel out', the output being seen in isolation, without concern for the quality of the information that went in to generating it."

🎓 Teaching Note: 5 Things to Show Beside a Gantt Chart When presenting a Gantt chart, identify: (1) assumptions, (2) estimate basis, (3) resource constraints, (4) change date, and (5) unresolved risks. Treat the plotted dates as a planned scenario that must be reviewed as evidence changes.
Slide 37 Slide 38
Software & Schedule Diagnosis

Use software for complexity it can represent & Diagnose a polished schedule

Useful Many tasks, frequent revisions, linked dates and consistent calculation.
Still human Judgement about scope, task logic, people, interfaces and uncertainty.
Practice Review the output with the team; update it when actual work diverges.

Slide 37 Note: Software can calculate a network accurately and still represent an unrealistic plan if the inputs are wrong.

Slide 38 Activity: Diagnose a Polished Schedule

• Scenario: A chart shows a 24-day finish. One analyst is booked on two simultaneous tasks, and a supplier has a 10-day lead time omitted from the network.

• Diagnosis Question: Which inputs fail: resource capacity, dependency, duration, or all three?

• Action Response: Add the supplier task and realistic availability, then recompute the network and dates.

➔ Suggested Answer: All three may change. The analyst conflict is capacity; supplier delivery is a missing activity and dependency; its lead time changes duration. Do not promise the original finish until the revised model is checked.

Slide 44 Beyond the Deterministic Network

What makes week 37 realistic?

1. Capacity

Can the same course staff prepare material while teaching other courses?

2. Calendar

Do holidays, application windows and room availability alter working weeks?

3. Uncertainty

Are publicity response, material approval and content changes represented?

🎓 Teaching Note The book asks for factors beyond the deterministic network. Students should identify specific assumptions and revise the schedule with actual resource and calendar information.

Exit Check: Explain the Chain

Slide 45 • Final Exam Self-Test & Suggested Responses

Slide 45 • Final Review Three High-Yield Exam Questions
1. How do PBS, WBS and OBS answer different questions?

➔ Suggested Answer: They represent three linked views of the project: PBS = product parts (what will be delivered?), WBS = work activities (what work must be done?, phrased as actions), and OBS = people / organisation (who is needed?, identifying potential human resources).

2. Why does a join use the maximum predecessor EF?

➔ Suggested Answer: Because in finish-to-start logic, a successor waits for every required predecessor to finish (ES = max[EF of all predecessors]), so the latest finishing predecessor controls when the successor can start.

3. Why can the critical path change, and why can a Gantt chart mislead?

➔ Suggested Answer: Criticality is not permanent—if durations or dependencies change, another path may become the longest path. A Gantt chart can mislead because a neat, polished chart may hide uncertainty, resource conflicts, or weak inputs ("Garbage in, Gospel out").
